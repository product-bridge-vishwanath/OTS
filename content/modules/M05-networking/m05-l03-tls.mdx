<Section kind="story">

A founder's app had been running fine for ten months on a VPS with Let's Encrypt, auto-renewing every 90 days via a cron job someone had set up when the server was first provisioned. On month eleven, every user's browser started showing `NET::ERR_CERT_DATE_INVALID` — a full-page scary warning, no way through it for most users.

The certificate hadn't failed to renew because Let's Encrypt was down, or because the domain had changed, or because of anything dramatic. The renewal script's cron job ran under a user account whose crontab had been silently dropped during an unrelated server migration eight months earlier — nobody had moved it, because nobody remembered it existed; it had been working so reliably that it had become invisible. Three renewals' worth of silence (roughly 270 days of a 90-day cycle) finally caught up at the exact moment the last valid certificate's 90-day clock ran out.

The fix took five minutes once found. The eight months of silent failure happened because a thing that "just works" and a thing that "is being monitored" are not the same claim, and nobody had been asking the second question.

</Section>

<Section kind="already-did">

You've seen the padlock icon in a browser's address bar and not thought about it, because that's the entire point — it's supposed to be invisible when working. You've probably also seen the alternative: a full-page red warning, `NET::ERR_CERT_DATE_INVALID` or similar, usually at the worst possible moment, usually with no warning beforehand unless someone was specifically watching for it.

If you've deployed to a modern PaaS, you've likely never set up TLS yourself at all — Vercel, Render and similar platforms provision and renew certificates automatically the moment you add a custom domain. This lesson is partly about understanding what that automation is doing for you, and partly about the VPS/self-managed case where nothing does it for you unless you've set it up — and kept it working.

</Section>

<Section kind="mental-model">

### What a certificate actually proves

A TLS certificate is a signed statement: *"the owner of `example.com`'s private key is the entity this certificate describes, and a Certificate Authority verified that at the time of issuance."* Two things follow directly from that sentence:

1. **"At the time of issuance" is why certificates expire.** A certificate issued a year ago made a claim about domain ownership *then*. Domains change hands; expiry forces periodic re-verification rather than trusting a years-old claim forever.
2. **It proves identity, not safety.** A certificate says "this is really `example.com`," not "this site is trustworthy" — a phishing site can have a perfectly valid certificate for its own, different domain. This is why a valid cert on the wrong domain is still a real attack, not a non-issue.

### Reading a real certificate

```bash
echo | openssl s_client -connect anthropic.com:443 -servername anthropic.com 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -subject -dates -issuer
```
```text
subject=CN=anthropic.com
notBefore=Nov  3 18:24:44 2026 GMT
notAfter=Feb  1 18:24:43 2027 GMT
issuer=C=US, O=Let's Encrypt, CN=YE2
```

Three facts, every time: **who** it's for (`subject`), **how long it's valid** (`notBefore`/`notAfter` — this one is a roughly 90-day Let's Encrypt certificate, their standard short lifetime, which is *why* automated renewal is load-bearing rather than optional), and **who vouches for it** (`issuer`).

<Callout type="key">
**Let's Encrypt's short (90-day) lifetime is a deliberate design choice, not a limitation** — it forces renewal automation to exist and be exercised constantly, rather than letting a manual yearly process quietly rot the one time nobody remembers to run it. The story's eight-month silent failure is exactly the failure mode short lifetimes are designed to surface quickly — and it still took that long, because the *monitoring* of the renewal job, not the renewal mechanism itself, was what was missing.
</Callout>

### Mixed content: the other common TLS-adjacent failure

A page loaded over `https://` that tries to load a script, image, or API call over plain `http://` gets blocked by the browser — "mixed content." This usually surfaces after a migration from HTTP to HTTPS where one hardcoded `http://` URL survived the move, often in a third-party embed or an old config value nobody re-checked.

<Operator>

```bash
# ── Inspect a real certificate's three facts ──────────────────────────
echo | openssl s_client -connect anthropic.com:443 -servername anthropic.com 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -subject -dates -issuer
#   Verified live: Let's Encrypt, ~90-day validity window.

# ── curl shows the same facts during a normal request ─────────────────
curl -vI https://anthropic.com 2>&1 | grep -iE "SSL connection|subject:|expire date|TLSv"
#   Verified: "SSL connection using TLSv1.3", subject and expire date lines.

# ── Calculate days until expiry — the number that should drive an alert ─
echo | openssl s_client -connect anthropic.com:443 -servername anthropic.com 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -enddate \
  | sed 's/notAfter=//' \
  | xargs -I{} date -j -f "%b %d %T %Y %Z" "{}" +%s 2>/dev/null \
  | xargs -I{} sh -c 'echo "days left: $(( ({} - $(date +%s)) / 86400 ))"'
#   On Linux, swap the `date -j -f` macOS syntax for: date -d "{}" +%s

# ── Check for mixed content risk after a migration ────────────────────
grep -rn "http://" --include="*.ts" --include="*.tsx" --include="*.html" . 2>/dev/null | head -10
#   Any hardcoded http:// in a codebase serving itself over https:// is a
#   mixed-content risk waiting for the day someone actually hits that path.
```

</Operator>

<Section kind="tools">

| Need | Tool |
|---|---|
| Automatic issuance + renewal (PaaS) | Vercel, Render, Netlify — zero setup, included |
| Automatic issuance + renewal (self-managed) | Caddy (renews automatically, zero config), certbot + Let's Encrypt |
| CDN-provided TLS | Cloudflare (terminates TLS at their edge, can even proxy to an HTTP-only origin — understand this trade-off before relying on it) |
| Inspect any certificate | `openssl s_client` + `openssl x509`, or just a browser's padlock → certificate details |
| Monitor expiry externally | UptimeRobot, Better Stack, or any uptime checker with a cert-expiry check — **external**, so it doesn't depend on the same server that might be the one failing |

The lesson from the story, generalized: **certificate renewal automation needs its own monitoring, separate from the renewal mechanism itself.** A cron job that silently stops running is indistinguishable from one that's working, right up until the certificate it was supposed to renew actually expires — which is M7.8's dead-man's-switch problem, wearing a TLS costume.

What good looks like: certificate expiry is monitored by something external to the server it's protecting, with an alert well before the 14-day mark; nobody has ever been surprised by `NET::ERR_CERT_DATE_INVALID` in production.

</Section>

<Section kind="hands-on">

1. **Inspect a real certificate** using the Operator block's `openssl` command, against your own domain if you have one, or a public site otherwise.

2. **Calculate days remaining** and compare it against whatever monitoring (if any) currently watches that number for your own product.

3. **Check your deployment for mixed content risk** — grep for hardcoded `http://` in a codebase that serves over `https://`.

4. **Write down, for your own product, who or what renews your certificate**, and what would happen if that mechanism silently stopped — is there anything that would notice before a user does?

</Section>

<Section kind="break-fix">

**Symptom:** A company's API works fine in the browser, in Postman, and from every developer's laptop. A specific enterprise customer's server-to-server integration fails every single call with a TLS handshake error — and has never once worked for them.

<Evidence title="the error, from the customer's side">

```text
SSL_ERROR_SELF_SIGNED_CERT_IN_CHAIN
  (or, from a Java client:)
PKIX path building failed: unable to find valid certification path
```

</Evidence>

<Evidence title="what the company's own checks show">

```text
$ curl -vI https://api.example.com 2>&1 | grep -iE "SSL connection|issuer"
* SSL connection using TLSv1.3
*  issuer: C=US; O=Let's Encrypt; CN=R3
$ echo | openssl s_client -connect api.example.com:443 2>/dev/null | openssl x509 -noout -issuer
issuer=C=US, O=Let's Encrypt, CN=R3
```

</Evidence>

<Evidence title="what the customer runs on their own locked-down server">

```text
$ curl -vI https://api.example.com
curl: (60) SSL certificate problem: unable to get local issuer certificate
```

</Evidence>

Everyone else's browser trusts this certificate instantly. This one customer's server, running the exact same TLS version, cannot. Why, and whose problem is it?

<Reveal label="Reveal the cause">

**The customer's server has an outdated or incomplete list of trusted root/intermediate certificates (a trust store), missing the specific intermediate CA this certificate chains up through — a problem invisible to browsers, which auto-update their trust stores, and very visible to locked-down enterprise servers, which often don't.**

Browsers (Chrome, Firefox, Safari) ship with frequently-updated root certificate trust stores and typically handle fetching missing intermediate certificates automatically. A locked-down enterprise Linux server, especially one that's been running for years without OS updates, often has a **stale** trust store — missing a root or intermediate certificate that was added or rotated more recently than the server's last update. `Let's Encrypt` has changed its intermediate CA chain over the years (this is a routine, documented part of their operation); a server whose trust store predates a given chain simply can't verify certificates issued under it, while every up-to-date client sails through.

**Why this is specifically a server-to-server problem and not a browser problem:** nothing is wrong with the certificate itself — issuer, dates, and domain match are all correct, as every check from the company's side confirms. The failure is entirely on the *verifying* side's knowledge of which CAs to trust, which is a property of that one machine's OS/trust-store maintenance, not of the certificate being presented.

**The fix, layered:**
1. **Confirm the full certificate chain is being served, not just the leaf certificate** — a common *server-side* misconfiguration that produces an identical-looking symptom: if the server only sends its own certificate without the intermediate(s), even an up-to-date client can fail, because it may not successfully fetch the missing intermediate itself. `openssl s_client -connect api.example.com:443 -showcerts` shows the full chain actually being sent — verify more than one certificate appears.
2. If the chain is complete and the symptom is genuinely isolated to this one customer, **it's their trust store, not your certificate** — point them at updating their OS/CA bundle (on most Linux systems, a `ca-certificates` package update).
3. Consider whether your CA's chain-of-trust choices affect your actual customer base's typical environment — enterprise-heavy customers running older, more conservative systems are exactly the segment most likely to hit this, which is a real factor in choosing a certificate authority for a B2B product.

**The transferable lesson:** "it works for me" and "it works for everyone" are different claims about TLS specifically, because trust is evaluated locally by each client against *its own* list of trusted authorities — a list that updates on its own schedule, which you don't control and can't verify remotely without asking.

</Reveal>

</Section>

<Section kind="checklist">

For any domain you operate:

- Is certificate renewal automated (Caddy, certbot with a cron, or a PaaS handling it entirely)?
- Is the renewal mechanism's HEALTH monitored externally — separately from the renewal mechanism itself?
- Does the server send its FULL certificate chain, not just the leaf cert? (`openssl s_client -showcerts`)
- Is there any hardcoded `http://` URL in a codebase served over `https://`?

What good looks like: nobody has ever been surprised by a certificate expiry in production, because something external watches the days-remaining number and alerts well before it matters.

Red flags: a renewal cron job with no monitoring of whether it actually ran; "it works for me" accepted as proof a TLS setup is correct for every client; a certificate chain never verified to be complete.

</Section>
