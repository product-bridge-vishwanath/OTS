<Section kind="story">

A team's dashboard had forty-one panels. It was beautiful. It had been built over a year by whoever happened to be curious about something: CPU per container, event-loop lag, Redis keyspace hits, a pie chart of HTTP methods, GC pause time, three different views of memory.

During their worst outage of the year — checkout down for fifty minutes — nobody looked at it. Afterwards, in the postmortem, someone asked why. The honest answer: *"I didn't know which panel would have told me."*

They rebuilt it with four panels: request rate, error rate, p95 latency, and database connections in use. The next incident was diagnosed in four minutes, because the error-rate panel went vertical at 14:04 and the connections panel was flat against its ceiling right next to it.

Forty-one panels was not ten times more observability than four. It was *less*, because none of them had been chosen to answer a question.

</Section>

<Section kind="already-did">

You've looked at metrics without calling them that: the invocation and duration graphs on your platform's dashboard, your database provider's connection and CPU charts, the request count on a Cloudflare overview page. You've probably also looked at one during an incident and not known whether what you were seeing was *normal* — which is the problem this lesson solves.

You may also have seen an average response time of 180ms and concluded things were fine, while a tenth of your users were waiting four seconds. Averages do that. The fix is percentiles, and it's the single most useful idea here.

</Section>

### The four golden signals

Almost any service's health is described by four numbers. They come from Google's SRE practice and they've earned their status: if you graph only four things, graph these.

| Signal | Question | For the demo app |
|---|---|---|
| **Traffic** | How much work is arriving? | requests/min by route; bookings created/min |
| **Errors** | What fraction fails? | 5xx ÷ total, by route |
| **Latency** | How long does it take? | <Term k="p95">p95</Term> and p99 per route, successes only |
| **Saturation** | How close to a limit? | Postgres connections ÷ 10, heap used ÷ limit, queue depth |

**Saturation is the one people omit and the one that pays.** Traffic, errors and latency tell you something is wrong *now*; saturation tells you something will be wrong soon. Connections climbing from 4 to 9 of 10 is a problem you can fix before any user sees an error — which is exactly the connection-starvation incident from L8.6 and M0.4, caught twelve minutes earlier.

### Why averages lie, and percentiles don't

Ten requests: nine take 100ms, one takes 5,000ms.

- **Mean:** 590ms — describes nobody. No request took 590ms.
- **p50 (median):** 100ms — the typical experience.
- **p95:** ~5,000ms — what your unluckiest users actually get.

<Callout type="key">
A mean can hide any amount of pain, because one fast path dilutes any number of slow ones. Always read p50 *and* p95 (or p99): p50 is the typical experience, p95 is the experience that generates support tickets. If p50 is flat and p95 is climbing, something is slow for a *subset* — a user with more data, one bad shard, one cold cache. That divergence is itself a diagnosis.
</Callout>

One subtlety that bites: **percentiles don't average.** You cannot take the p95 of each instance and average them to get the fleet p95 — that number is meaningless. Aggregate the underlying distribution (that's what histograms are for), or read p95 per instance separately.

### Counters, gauges, histograms

| Type | Behaviour | Example | Read it as |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Counter** | Only increases | total requests, total errors | a **rate** — `rate(errors[5m])` |
| **Gauge** | Up and down | connections in use, queue depth, memory | the current value |
| **Histogram** | Distribution of values in buckets | request duration | percentiles |

The common mistake is graphing a counter raw: "total requests: 48,211,903" is a line that only goes up and tells you nothing. Counters must be differentiated into rates before they mean anything.

### Dashboard design: four panels, one question each

Rules that keep a dashboard usable at 3am:

1. **One screen, no scrolling.** If you must scroll, you'll miss the correlation that explains the incident.
2. **Four to six panels.** Traffic, errors, latency, saturation — then at most two things specific to your product.
3. **Same time range across all panels**, so corners line up visually. That alignment *is* the diagnosis: error rate going vertical next to connections hitting a ceiling tells the story without a word.
4. **Label axes and state the unit.** "4.2" is not a measurement.
5. **Mark deploys on the time axis.** Half of all incidents correlate with a deploy, and this answers debugging-method step 5 instantly.
6. **Every panel must answer a question you'd actually ask.** If nobody can say what action a panel's movement would trigger, delete it. That's the forty-one-panel lesson.

<Operator>

You can get all four signals from the demo app with no new infrastructure — the database and your logs already hold them.

```bash
cd demo-app
node scripts/load/steady-browse-and-order.js &

# ── TRAFFIC: requests per route, last 5 minutes ──────────────────────
docker compose logs --since 5m app 2>&1 \
  | grep -oE "(GET|POST|PATCH) /api/[a-z-]*" | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn

# ── ERRORS: as a RATIO, not a count ──────────────────────────────────
total=$(docker compose logs --since 5m app 2>&1 | grep -cE " (2|4|5)[0-9]{2} ")
errs=$(docker compose logs --since 5m app 2>&1  | grep -cE " 5[0-9]{2} ")
echo "error rate: $errs / $total"

# ── LATENCY: percentiles from logged durations ───────────────────────
docker compose logs --since 5m app 2>&1 | grep -oE "[0-9]+ms" | tr -d 'ms' | sort -n \
  | awk '{a[NR]=$1} END {
      printf "n=%d p50=%dms p95=%dms p99=%dms max=%dms\n",
      NR, a[int(NR*0.50)], a[int(NR*0.95)], a[int(NR*0.99)], a[NR]}'
#   Compare that p95 with the mean to see the lie for yourself:
docker compose logs --since 5m app 2>&1 | grep -oE "[0-9]+ms" | tr -d 'ms' \
  | awk '{s+=$1; n++} END {printf "mean=%.0fms over %d requests\n", s/n, n}'

# ── SATURATION: the leading indicator ────────────────────────────────
docker compose exec -T db psql -U ots -d demo -tAc "
  SELECT count(*) || ' / ' || current_setting('max_connections') AS connections
    FROM pg_stat_activity WHERE datname='demo';"
docker compose exec -T db psql -U ots -d demo -c "
  SELECT numbackends, xact_rollback, blks_hit, blks_read,
         round(100.0*blks_hit/nullif(blks_hit+blks_read,0),1) AS cache_hit_pct
    FROM pg_stat_database WHERE datname='demo';"
docker stats --no-stream --format "table {{.Name}}\t{{.CPUPerc}}\t{{.MemUsage}}\t{{.MemPerc}}"
docker compose exec -T cache redis-cli info memory | grep -E "used_memory_human|maxmemory_human"

# ── Postgres metrics worth a panel each ──────────────────────────────
docker compose exec -T db psql -U ots -d demo -c "
  SELECT relname, seq_scan, idx_scan, n_live_tup
    FROM pg_stat_user_tables ORDER BY seq_scan DESC LIMIT 5;"
#   seq_scan rising on a growing table predicts the missing-index incident.

# ── Watch saturation move BEFORE errors appear ───────────────────────
node scripts/break/connection-hungry-backfill.js 12 2 &
for i in $(seq 1 10); do
  c=$(docker compose exec -T db psql -U ots -d demo -tAc \
      "SELECT count(*) FROM pg_stat_activity WHERE datname='demo';")
  e=$(docker compose logs --since 20s app 2>&1 | grep -cE " 5[0-9]{2} ")
  echo "t=${i}0s connections=$c errors_last_20s=$e"
  sleep 10
done
kill %2 2>/dev/null
```

That last loop is the whole argument for saturation metrics: watch the connection count climb for a minute or more while the error count is still zero. That gap is your warning window.

<Section kind="tools">

| Tool | Model | Good for | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Platform metrics** (Vercel/Render/Railway) | Pre-built | Free, instant, covers traffic/errors/latency | No custom metrics; short history on cheap tiers |
| **Prometheus + Grafana** | Pull from `/metrics` | The standard; self-hosted; powerful | You run it; PromQL has a learning curve |
| **Grafana Cloud** | Hosted Prometheus | Same without running it | Free tier is generous, then not |
| **Datadog / New Relic** | Agent-based | Everything, correlated, fast setup | Cost scales alarmingly with hosts and custom metrics |
| **Database built-ins** (`pg_stat_*`) | SQL queries | Saturation and query metrics for free | You must scrape them into something |
| **Sentry** | Error-first + performance | Error rate and slow transactions without a dashboard | Not a general metrics store |

Minimum viable metrics for a small product, in order:

1. **Your platform's built-in traffic/error/latency panels.** Free, already there.
2. **One saturation number scraped on a schedule** — database connections in use is almost always the right one. A cron writing it to a table beats not having it by a wide margin.
3. **Deploy markers** on your graphs, however crude.
4. **Prometheus/Grafana or Grafana Cloud** when you need custom business metrics (bookings/min) and real history.

A `/metrics` endpoint, if you go the Prometheus route — note the label discipline:

```ts
import { Registry, Counter, Histogram, Gauge } from "prom-client";
export const registry = new Registry();

export const httpRequests = new Counter({
  name: "http_requests_total",
  help: "HTTP requests",
  labelNames: ["route", "method", "status"],   // route PATTERN, bounded values
  registers: [registry],
});

export const httpDuration = new Histogram({
  name: "http_request_duration_seconds",
  help: "Request duration",
  labelNames: ["route", "method"],
  buckets: [0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 10],   // choose around your SLO
  registers: [registry],
});

export const dbConnectionsInUse = new Gauge({
  name: "db_connections_in_use",
  help: "Pool connections currently checked out",
  registers: [registry],
});
```

Label rules, since this is where real money is lost: **route patterns, not raw URLs** (`/api/bookings/:id`), and never `user_id`, `request_id`, `email` or a timestamp. Each distinct combination is a stored time series; unbounded labels mean an unbounded bill and a dashboard that times out (L8.1's <Term k="cardinality">cardinality</Term> trap).

What good looks like: one screen, four to six panels, all four golden signals, aligned time ranges, deploy markers, and labelled units — openable by anyone on the team during an incident without a guided tour.

<Section kind="break-fix">

**Symptom:** Monday 09:00, the start of the week's traffic. The team's dashboard is green on every panel. Support is receiving a steady trickle of "the page won't load" reports — perhaps one in twenty users. Nobody can reproduce it.

<Evidence title="the dashboard, as configured">

```text
Panel 1  "Avg response time"        → 212 ms     (flat, normal)
Panel 2  "Total requests (today)"   → 48,211     (rising line, as every day)
Panel 3  "Errors (count, 1h)"       → 38         (was 31 last Monday — "normal-ish")
Panel 4  "CPU %"                    → 23%        (flat)
Panel 5  "Memory %"                 → 61%        (flat)
Panel 6  "HTTP methods (pie)"       → 71% GET    (unchanged)
```

</Evidence>

<Evidence title="what the raw data shows when someone finally computes it">

```text
$ latency percentiles, last 15 min, /api/bookings only
  n=2,214  p50=104ms  p95=4,870ms  p99=30,012ms  max=30,041ms

$ error ratio by route, last 15 min
  /api/rooms     0 / 1,602   (0.0%)
  /api/bookings  38 / 612    (6.2%)

$ saturation (not on any panel)
  SELECT count(*) FROM pg_stat_activity WHERE datname='demo';  →  10
  SHOW max_connections;                                        →  10
  pool.waiting (from app logs)                                 →  sawtooth 3–9

$ first error timestamp: 08:58:41    (traffic ramp begins ~08:50)
```

</Evidence>

Every panel is green and 6.2% of the key endpoint is failing. Which panel designs caused the blindness, and what are the four replacements?

<Reveal label="Reveal the cause">

**The underlying fault is connection-pool saturation under Monday's traffic ramp — but the lesson here is that every one of the six panels was designed in a way that cannot show it.**

**Panel by panel, the design errors:**

1. **"Avg response time" — 212ms, flat.** The mean is diluted by 1,602 fast cached `/api/rooms` hits. The real distribution is p50 104ms / **p99 30 seconds** — a third of a minute, i.e. requests dying at the pool timeout. The average literally cannot show a tail. *Replace with p50 + p95 + p99.*
2. **"Total requests (today)" — a raw counter.** It only ever goes up, so it looks identical on a healthy and an unhealthy day. *Replace with a rate (requests/min), which makes the 08:50 ramp visible.*
3. **"Errors (count, 1h)" — 38 vs 31 last week.** A count without traffic context is uninterpretable: 38 errors spread across 2,214 requests reads as noise, while 38 across the 612 requests to *one* route is 6.2% — an outage for that endpoint. Aggregating all routes together hid it completely. *Replace with error ratio, broken down by route.*
4. **CPU and memory — flat and irrelevant.** Waiting for a database connection consumes neither. These are the classic "saturation metrics of the wrong resource": they measure the host, while the limiting resource is the connection pool. *Replace with saturation of the actual constraint — connections in use ÷ max, and pool waiters.*
5. **The HTTP-methods pie chart** answers no question anyone would ask during an incident. *Delete.*

**What the four panels would have shown:** error ratio for `/api/bookings` jumping to 6.2% at 08:58, p99 pinned at the 30-second timeout, request rate ramping from 08:50, and connections flat against 10 — with the corners vertically aligned. That's a four-minute diagnosis, as in the story.

**And the fix the metrics point to:** `max_connections = 10` with a pool of the same size is simply too small for Monday's concurrency — so some requests always wait, and under ramp they wait past the timeout. Raise the limit *and* add a pooler (M6.2), with an alert on connections > 80% so the warning arrives before the errors.

**The transferable rules, stated plainly:**
- **Percentiles, never means.** A mean cannot represent a tail, and the tail is what users feel.
- **Ratios, never counts.** An error count is uninterpretable without traffic.
- **Break down by route.** A healthy aggregate routinely hides one broken endpoint.
- **Measure the saturation of the *limiting* resource**, which is rarely CPU in a web app — it's connections, memory, queue depth or a provider's rate limit.
- **Delete panels that answer no question.** They don't just waste space; they create the false confidence that made this team ignore their own dashboard.

</Reveal>

<Section kind="checklist">

For every service, know these four:

- **Traffic** — requests per minute, by route
- **Errors** — error *ratio*, by route
- **Latency** — p50 and p95 (or p99), successes separated from failures
- **Saturation** — the limiting resource as a fraction of its limit

Dashboard review questions:

- Does it fit on one screen without scrolling?
- Do all panels share a time range so corners align?
- Are deploys marked on the time axis?
- Does every panel have a stated action if it moves? (If not, delete it.)
- Is any panel showing a mean where a percentile belongs?
- Is any panel showing a count where a ratio belongs?
- Is the saturation panel measuring the resource that actually limits you?

Metric hygiene:

- Counters read as rates, never raw.
- Route patterns as labels, never raw URLs or user ids.
- Histogram buckets chosen around your target, not library defaults.

What good looks like: four to six panels, aligned, labelled, each with an action — and a saturation alert that fires before users notice.

Red flags: forty-one panels nobody opens; average response time as the headline; error counts without traffic; CPU as your only saturation metric.

</Section>
