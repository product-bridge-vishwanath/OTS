<Section kind="story">

An engineer joined a four-person startup and was handed the on-call phone in week two. The first page came at 23:40: checkout failing.

She knew exactly what to do — read the logs. It took her **nineteen minutes to find them.** The web app's logs were in Vercel's dashboard. The worker's logs were on Render, a different account she didn't have access to. The database's slow queries were in Supabase, under a tab called "Logs & Analytics" that defaulted to a different log type. The payment webhook failures were in Stripe's own dashboard. And a `console.log` the previous developer had added was writing to a file on a VPS nobody had mentioned.

The error, when she finally found it, had been sitting in the Stripe dashboard the whole time, in plain English.

Nineteen minutes of an outage spent on *navigation*. The fix for this is not a tool — it's a list, written down before the incident, of where each box's logs live.

</Section>

<Section kind="already-did">

You've read logs in at least three places: your platform's log tab, your terminal during `npm run dev`, and a provider's dashboard. You may have hit the two classic walls without naming them: logs that **disappeared** (platform retention, or a container that was replaced), and logs that were **unsearchable** because everything interesting was inside a wall of text.

You've also almost certainly done the thing that makes logs hard to find later: `console.log` in a `catch` block. It works beautifully in development, where you're watching. In production it goes wherever that process's stdout goes — which may be nowhere you ever look.

</Section>

<Section kind="mental-model">

The governing rule, and nearly everything follows from it:

<Callout type="key">
**Your app should write logs to stdout and nothing else.** Not to a file, not to a log service directly. Whatever runs your process — Docker, systemd, your platform — captures stdout and routes it. An app that writes its own log files breaks in containers (the file vanishes with the container), fills disks (M1.3's break-fix), and can't be aggregated.
</Callout>

So "where are the logs?" is really "what captured stdout, and where did it put it?"

### The six places logs hide

| Where | How to read it | Retention | The gotcha |
|---|---|---|---|
| Platform log tab | Dashboard, or `vercel logs` / `railway logs` | Hours to days | **Short.** Last week may be gone forever |
| `docker logs` | `docker compose logs -f app` | Until the container is removed | `docker compose down` **deletes them** |
| systemd journal | `journalctl -u app --since "1 hour ago"` | Disk-limited, often weeks | Needs `--no-pager` in scripts |
| A file on a server | `tail -f /var/log/app/app.log` | Until the disk fills | The anti-pattern; fills disks |
| Provider dashboards | Stripe, Supabase, Resend, Anthropic each have their own | Varies, often generous | **The forgotten one** — where the story's answer was |
| Aggregator | Query language (KQL, LogQL, Datadog search) | What you pay for | Setup cost; cost scales with volume |

The row people miss is **provider dashboards**. When a third-party call fails, *their* logs usually explain it better than yours: Stripe tells you exactly why a charge was declined; Resend shows you the bounce reason; Anthropic shows you the rate-limit window. Your log line says "request failed".

### Retention is the constraint that decides what you can learn

A log you can't read is a log you don't have. Three failure modes:

1. **Expired** — platform retention is often 1–7 days. Monday's incident is unanswerable by Friday.
2. **Destroyed** — `docker compose down`, a container replacement, or a redeploy can take logs with them.
3. **Unsearchable** — present but unstructured, so finding anything requires reading rather than querying (L8.3 fixes this).

The practical response: during an incident, **save the evidence before you fix anything.** The fix often destroys the logs — a restart replaces the container, a redeploy rolls the platform's log window. Copy what you need to a file first; it costs five seconds and is routinely the difference between a postmortem and a shrug.

### How to search, in four moves

The same four work in `grep`, Kibana, Loki and Datadog — only the syntax changes.

| Move | `grep` | Kibana / Loki / Datadog |
|---|---|---|
| Narrow by **time** first | `--since 30m` | time picker — always set this first |
| Filter by **field** | `grep 'status=500'` | `status:500` |
| Find the **first** occurrence | `… \| head -5` | sort ascending |
| Get **context** around a hit | `grep -B3 -A10` | "surrounding documents" / `| context` |

Time first, then field, then first-occurrence, then context. Searching a whole retention window for the word "error" is how you end up reading eleven thousand lines, as in M1.3's story.

<Operator>

```bash
cd demo-app

# ── Follow live (deploys, reproducing a bug) ──────────────────────────
docker compose logs -f app
docker compose logs -f --tail 20 app worker          # several services, interleaved
#   Platform / VPS equivalents:
vercel logs --follow ; railway logs ; flyctl logs
journalctl -u riverside -f

# ── Time window first, ALWAYS ────────────────────────────────────────
docker compose logs --since 30m --timestamps app
docker compose logs --since 2026-11-07T14:00:00 --until 2026-11-07T14:10:00 app
journalctl -u riverside -S "14:00" -U "14:10" --no-pager

# ── Then filter, then find the FIRST occurrence ──────────────────────
docker compose logs --since 1h app 2>&1 | grep -iE "error|fatal" | head -5
docker compose logs --since 1h app 2>&1 | grep -c -iE "error"      # how many?

# ── Context around a hit (a stack trace lives below its first line) ──
docker compose logs --since 1h app 2>&1 | grep -A12 -B2 "Error:" | head -40

# ── Several services: who saw the request? (correlation by hand) ─────
docker compose logs --since 10m app worker 2>&1 | grep "booking.created"
#   This is painful without request ids — which is exactly L8.3's point.

# ── SAVE EVIDENCE BEFORE FIXING (the fix destroys the logs) ──────────
mkdir -p /tmp/incident-$(date +%F)
docker compose logs --since 2h --timestamps app  > /tmp/incident-$(date +%F)/app.log
docker compose logs --since 2h --timestamps worker > /tmp/incident-$(date +%F)/worker.log
docker compose ps > /tmp/incident-$(date +%F)/containers.txt
docker compose exec -T db psql -U ots -d demo -c \
  "SELECT * FROM pg_stat_activity WHERE datname='demo';" \
  > /tmp/incident-$(date +%F)/db-activity.txt
#   Now you can restart freely.

# ── Provider-side logs (the forgotten source) ────────────────────────
#   No CLI needed — bookmark these, one per third-party box on your map:
#     Stripe    → Developers → Events / Logs   (why a charge failed)
#     Supabase  → Logs & Analytics → Postgres  (slow queries, errors)
#     Resend    → Emails → Logs                (bounce reasons)
#     Anthropic → Console → Usage/Logs         (rate limits, spend)

# ── Database logs: the ones nobody checks ────────────────────────────
docker compose logs --since 30m db 2>&1 | grep -iE "error|fatal|deadlock|slow" | head
docker compose exec -T db psql -U ots -d demo -c "
  SELECT query, state, now() - query_start AS running_for
    FROM pg_stat_activity
   WHERE datname='demo' AND state <> 'idle' ORDER BY query_start LIMIT 5;"
```

The `--timestamps` flag matters more than it looks: without it, `docker logs` output has no time information at all, which makes timeline reconstruction (debugging-method step 3) impossible.

</Operator>

<Section kind="tools">

| Tool | Query style | When it's the right call |
|---|---|---|
| Platform log tab | Text search, basic filters | Always your first stop; zero setup |
| `journalctl` | Flags (`-u`, `-S`, `-p`) | On a VPS; genuinely good once you know four flags |
| `docker compose logs` | Flags + pipe to `grep` | Local and single-host container work |
| **Axiom / Better Stack** | Structured query | Small teams wanting real search cheaply |
| **Grafana Loki** | LogQL — `{app="web"} |= "error"` | You already run Grafana for metrics |
| **Datadog / New Relic** | Rich query + correlation | Funded teams, many services; expensive at volume |
| **CloudWatch Logs** | Logs Insights SQL-ish | You're on AWS and it's already there |
| Kibana / OpenSearch | KQL — `status:500 and route:"/api/bookings"` | Self-hosted ELK, or inherited one |

Enough KQL and LogQL to be dangerous, since these two cover most inherited setups:

```text
# Kibana (KQL)
status:500 and route:"/api/bookings" and not user_agent:*bot*
@timestamp >= "2026-11-07T14:00:00" and level:error

# Loki (LogQL)
{service="app"} |= "error" | json | status >= 500
sum(rate({service="app"} |= "error" [5m]))     # a metric, derived from logs
```

What good looks like: **a written list, one line per box on your L0.3 map, of where its logs live and how far back they go.** Four lines for a small product. That list is the nineteen minutes the engineer in the story lost, recovered.

| Box | Where | Retention |
|---|---|---|
| Web app | Vercel → project → Logs (`vercel logs`) | 1 day (Hobby) |
| Worker | Render → service → Logs | 7 days |
| Postgres | Supabase → Logs & Analytics → Postgres | 1 day |
| Payments | Stripe → Developers → Events | 30+ days |

<Section kind="hands-on">

```bash
cd demo-app && docker compose up -d --build
node scripts/load/steady-browse-and-order.js &
```

1. **Follow live, then reproduce a failure while watching.** In one terminal `docker compose logs -f app`; in another `docker compose stop db`. Watch the failure appear in real time, then `docker compose start db`.
2. **Practise time-first searching:**
   ```bash
   docker compose logs --since 5m --timestamps app | tail -20
   docker compose logs --since 5m app 2>&1 | grep -iE "error" | head -3
   ```
3. **Get context around a stack trace** — note that the useful lines are *below* the match:
   ```bash
   docker compose logs app 2>&1 | grep -A10 "Error:" | head -24
   ```
4. **Read a log source you probably never check** — the database's own:
   ```bash
   docker compose logs --since 10m db 2>&1 | tail -20
   ```
5. **Prove that logs are destroyed by routine operations.** Count lines, recreate the container, count again:
   ```bash
   docker compose logs app 2>&1 | wc -l
   docker compose up -d --force-recreate app
   docker compose logs app 2>&1 | wc -l        # the history is gone
   ```
   This is why evidence capture comes *before* the fix.
6. **Do the evidence-capture drill.** Trigger a real failure, save everything, *then* fix:
   ```bash
   node scripts/break/connection-hungry-backfill.js 15 2 &
   sleep 25
   mkdir -p /tmp/ots-incident
   docker compose logs --since 5m --timestamps app > /tmp/ots-incident/app.log
   docker compose exec -T db psql -U ots -d demo -c \
     "SELECT count(*), state FROM pg_stat_activity WHERE datname='demo' GROUP BY state;" \
     > /tmp/ots-incident/db.txt
   kill %2 2>/dev/null; head -5 /tmp/ots-incident/app.log; cat /tmp/ots-incident/db.txt
   ```
7. **Write your own log-location table** for your real product, using the four-column format above. This is the deliverable of the lesson.

<Section kind="break-fix">

**Symptom:** Customers report that booking confirmation emails stopped arriving this morning. Bookings themselves work fine — rows appear in the database. The team's app logs show nothing unusual.

<Evidence title="app logs (the only place they looked)">

```text
$ docker compose logs --since 3h app 2>&1 | grep -iE "email|mail|resend" | head
(no output)

$ docker compose logs --since 3h app 2>&1 | grep -iE "error|fatal" | head
(no output)

$ docker compose logs --since 3h app 2>&1 | tail -4
booking.created id=5841 room=1 2026-11-09 10-13
GET /api/bookings 200 194ms
booking.created id=5842 room=3 2026-11-09 14-16
GET /api/rooms 200 5ms (cache: hit)
```

</Evidence>

<Evidence title="what the worker's logs show">

```text
$ docker compose logs --since 3h worker 2>&1 | tail -6
[worker] report run starting at 2026-11-09T09:00:04.113Z
[worker] report written: Bookings today: 12. Projected revenue: $2,840.00.
[worker] email batch: 12 queued
[worker] email batch: sending via provider
[worker] email batch: done
[worker] report run starting at 2026-11-09T09:10:03.981Z
```

</Evidence>

<Evidence title="the provider's dashboard (checked 40 minutes later)">

```text
Resend → Emails → Logs, last 3 hours:
  Delivered:  0
  Bounced:    0
  Failed:     47
  Status detail on each: "403 — domain riverside-studios.example is not verified.
                          Verification record (TXT _resend) was removed from DNS."

Resend → Domains:
  riverside-studios.example   ⚠ Not verified   (verified until 2026-11-09 08:52)
```

</Evidence>

<Evidence title="change log">

```text
$ Cloudflare DNS audit log:
  2026-11-09 08:51  TXT  _resend  DELETED  by asha@…
  2026-11-09 08:50  TXT  _dmarc   UPDATED  by asha@…
  (ticket: "clean up unused DNS records")
```

</Evidence>

The app logged nothing wrong, and it was telling the truth. Where was the answer, and what does that teach about log sources?

<Reveal label="Reveal the cause">

**A DNS cleanup removed Resend's domain-verification TXT record, so the provider began rejecting every send with a 403. The answer was in the *provider's* logs — a source the team never opened.**

**Why their own logs were blameless and useless.** The worker's log says `email batch: done`, which is honest: it successfully handed 12 messages to the provider's API. If the code only checks for a transport-level failure (or ignores the response entirely), a per-message `403` in the provider's response body never becomes an error in their system. Their logs correctly record "we sent them"; only Resend knows they were refused. This is the forgotten-source row of the table, and it's where third-party failures almost always live.

**Reading the evidence in the right order:**
1. The symptom is in a third-party box (email), so the cheapest check is that provider's dashboard — and it gives the cause *in plain English*, including the exact record name.
2. The DNS audit log supplies the event: `TXT _resend DELETED` at 08:51, minutes before the first failure. "Clean up unused DNS records" removed a record that looked unused and was load-bearing.
3. Verification expiring at 08:52 matches the timeline precisely.

**Fix, in order:**
1. **Restore the TXT record** in Cloudflare and re-verify the domain in Resend. Deliverability returns within the record's propagation window.
2. **Resend the 47 failures** — deliberately and once, checking for duplicates, because some may have been retried already (M7.7's idempotency question).
3. **Fix the blind spot, which is the real cause.** The worker must inspect the provider's per-message response and log a *distinguishable* error on rejection:
   `email.failed to=… provider_status=403 reason="domain not verified"`. Then add an alert on `email.failed > 0`, and a metric for delivered-vs-attempted.
4. **Add provider dashboards to the log-location list** from this lesson, one line per third-party box — Stripe, Resend, Anthropic. Bookmark them in one folder so the cheapest check is also the easiest.
5. Separately: a DNS record deletion that breaks a verification is an argument for naming such records in a comment, or for noting them on the architecture map as load-bearing.

**The transferable lesson:** "our logs show nothing" does not mean "nothing is wrong" — it frequently means **you are reading the wrong system's logs.** Every arrow on your map that leaves your infrastructure has a log on the other side, and that log usually explains the failure better than yours ever could.

</Reveal>

<Section kind="checklist">

The questions that make logs findable:

- Where does each box's stdout actually go? (Write the table: box → location → retention.)
- How far back can I search, per source? Which incidents are already unanswerable?
- Does any of my code write log files instead of stdout?
- Do I have the provider dashboards bookmarked for every third-party box on my map?
- Do I capture evidence *before* restarting or redeploying?
- Are timestamps included in my log output? (`--timestamps` for Docker; many formats omit them.)

The search method, in order: **time window → field filter → first occurrence → context around it.**

What good looks like: anyone on the team can find any box's logs in under a minute, from a written list, at 3am.

Red flags: nineteen minutes of an incident spent navigating; "our logs show nothing" accepted as "nothing is wrong"; `docker compose down` run during an incident (it deletes the evidence); an app writing its own log files.

</Section>
