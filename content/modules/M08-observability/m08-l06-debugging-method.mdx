<Section kind="story">

Friday, 6:40 pm. A founder ships a small copy change through her AI agent and goes to dinner. At 7:15 the first customer email arrives: *"checkout is broken."* She opens the site — it loads. She tries checkout — spinner, then an error page. She pastes the error into her agent. It confidently replaces the payment library call. Checkout is still broken. She pastes the new error. The agent adds a retry. Still broken, now slower. By 9 pm she has made four deploys, each one a guess, and checkout has been down for two hours.

The actual cause: the 6:40 deploy picked up a new environment, and one variable — `STRIPE_WEBHOOK_SECRET` — wasn't set there. The first error message said so, on line 14 of the <Term k="stack-trace">stack trace</Term>. Nobody read line 14. The agent only ever saw the one line she pasted.

Two hours of outage, zero of which were spent debugging. All of it was spent *guessing*.

</Section>

<Section kind="already-did">

You have done some version of this: an error appears, you copy the last red line, paste it into the agent, and apply whatever it suggests. Sometimes that works — the agent is good at recognising common errors. But notice what you were actually doing: you were using the agent as a **slot machine**. Pull the lever, hope the fix comes out. When it works you learn nothing, and when it doesn't you pull again.

What was missing isn't intelligence — the agent has plenty, and so do you. What was missing is a **method**: a fixed sequence that turns "it's broken" into "I know which layer it's in, what changed, and what evidence I have." Engineers who look like wizards during incidents aren't smarter. They just refuse to skip steps.

This lesson is the method. Every other lesson in this module gives you the instruments (logs, metrics, traces); this one tells you the order in which to use them.

</Section>

<Section kind="mental-model">

The method is eight steps. The first five are diagnosis, and you are not allowed to touch the fix until they're done.

**The analogy.** A good doctor doesn't prescribe from the first word of your complaint. They take a history (*timeline*), examine you (*reproduce, read the evidence*), rule out systems one by one (*isolate the layer*), and ask what's new — travel, food, medication (*what changed?*). Prescribing on the first symptom is malpractice. It's malpractice in production too.

### The steps

1. **Reproduce.** Make it fail in front of you, ideally with one `curl` or one click. If you can't reproduce it, you can't confirm any fix — you can only re-deploy and pray. "Sometimes" failures get a frequency instead: *3 of 10 attempts fail*.
2. **Read the actual error — all of it.** Not the toast in the UI, not the summary, not the last line. The real error in the logs, with its full <Term k="stack-trace">stack trace</Term>. The cause is usually stated in plain text and is routinely ignored: `ECONNREFUSED`, `relation "orders" does not exist`, `is not defined`. The UI shows the costume; the log shows the actor.
3. **Build a timeline.** When did it *start*? Error trackers and log timestamps answer this precisely. "It started at 18:42" is the single most valuable fact you can collect, because step 5 lives or dies by it.
4. **Isolate the layer.** Every request passes through the same five neighbourhoods: **client → network → app → data → third party**. Evidence tells you which one to search. You don't need to know *what* is broken yet — only *where*.
5. **What changed?** Systems that worked yesterday don't break spontaneously. Something changed: a deploy, a config or env var, a dependency, the data (growth, a weird record), the traffic, or a third party's side. Cross-reference the change list against the timeline from step 3.
6. **Fix the cause, not the symptom.** A retry around a failing call, a `try/catch` that swallows the error, a bigger timeout — these make dashboards greener while the disease spreads. The test: *can you explain, in one sentence, why the system failed and why this fix stops that mechanism?* If not, it's a patch.
7. **Confirm.** Run the exact reproduction from step 1 and watch it pass. Then check you didn't break the neighbours (the surrounding pages, the logs staying quiet).
8. **Write it down.** Three lines: symptom, cause, fix. This becomes your incident log, your runbook seed, and — in this program — your personal <Term k="rollback">failure catalogue</Term>.

<Callout type="key">
The order is the method. Diagnosis (1–5) before treatment (6–8), always. Every production horror story in this course is someone running step 6 first.
</Callout>

### Isolating the layer: what the evidence says

| Evidence you see | Layer to search | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Error in the browser console, network tab shows **no request sent** | Client | The code in the browser failed before calling anyone |
| Request sent, response is <Term k="five-oh-two">502</Term>/504 from the proxy | App (behind the <Term k="reverse-proxy">proxy</Term>) | The proxy is fine — its <Term k="upstream">upstream</Term> didn't answer |
| Request sent, times out, *some* users affected by region | Network / DNS / CDN | The app never saw the request |
| App logs show the request arriving, then a DB error | Data | The app is fine; its dependency refused |
| App logs show a slow or failing call to `api.stripe.com` etc. | Third party | Check their status page *before* touching your code |
| Started at the exact minute of a deploy | App (what shipped) | Timeline beats intuition |

<Operator>

The five questions, as commands. Run them top to bottom and the layer falls out:

```bash
# Is it reachable at all? (network vs app)
curl -sv https://yourapp.com/api/health -o /dev/null

# What does the edge say vs the app itself? (proxy vs upstream)
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code} %{time_total}s\n" https://yourapp.com/api/checkout

# What is the app actually logging right now?
#   PaaS: open the log tab, or stream:
vercel logs --follow        # Vercel
railway logs                # Railway
#   VPS / systemd:
journalctl -u myapp -S "18:30" --no-pager | tail -50
#   Docker:
docker logs --since 30m myapp 2>&1 | grep -iE "error|fatal" | tail -20

# When did it start? (timeline)
journalctl -u myapp --no-pager | grep -i error | head -5   # first occurrence, not last

# What changed? (deploys + config)
git log --oneline --since="6 hours ago"
vercel ls   # or your platform's deploy history, next to the env-var change log
```

Habit worth stealing: find the **first** error in the window, not the most recent. Later errors are usually fallout from the first one.

</Operator>

<Section kind="tools">

The method is tool-independent — these just make steps 2–3 faster. You met most of them earlier in this module.

| Tool | What it gives the method | Step it accelerates | Typical setup |
|---|---|---|---|
| Platform log tab (Vercel / Render / Railway) | The actual error text, zero setup | 2 · Read the error | Built in |
| Sentry (error tracking) | First-seen time, frequency, full stack trace, release that introduced it | 2 & 3 & 5 | One SDK line |
| Grafana / platform metrics | "When did latency/errors jump?" as a picture | 3 · Timeline | Dashboards per service |
| `curl -v` | Layer isolation from your own terminal | 4 · Isolate | Nothing |
| Deploy history + env-var audit log | The change list | 5 · What changed | Built into your platform |

What good looks like: when something breaks, you can get from symptom to *layer + timeline* in under ten minutes using only the tools above. If any step needs a tool you don't have (no error tracker, no deploy history), that's a gap to close this week, not during the next incident.

</Section>

<Section kind="hands-on">

Do this on a product you actually run (or the demo repo if you prefer).

1. **Break it on purpose.** In a non-production environment, rename one required environment variable (e.g. `DATABASE_URL` → `DATABASE_URL_X`) and restart/redeploy.
2. **Run the method, writing each step down as you go:**
   - Reproduce: which URL or action fails? What exactly do you see?
   - Read: find the *full* error in the logs — not the browser. Copy the line that names the cause.
   - Timeline: find the first occurrence timestamp.
   - Layer: which of the five layers is it in, and which single piece of evidence told you?
   - What changed: your own change, found via the platform's deploy/config history — *pretend you don't remember doing it* and locate the change the way you'd have to in a real incident.
3. **Fix it, confirm with the same reproduction, and write the three-line writeup**: symptom / cause / fix.
4. Put the writeup somewhere permanent. That's entry #1 of your personal failure catalogue.

The exercise feels artificial exactly once. The first real incident after it will not.

</Section>

<Section kind="break-fix">

**Symptom:** since about 14:00, roughly a third of requests to `/api/orders` return a 500. The rest are fine. The AI agent, shown one error message, has proposed "adding retry logic to the orders endpoint." Before you let it: run the method on the evidence below.

<Evidence title="app logs (excerpt, 14:02–14:04)">

```text
14:02:11 INFO  req_8f21 GET /api/orders 200 212ms
14:02:14 INFO  req_8f22 GET /api/orders 200 198ms
14:02:19 ERROR req_8f25 GET /api/orders 500 30004ms
  Error: Connection terminated due to connection timeout
      at Connection.<anonymous> (node_modules/pg/lib/client.js:132)
      at Timeout._onTimeout (node_modules/pg-pool/index.js:45)
14:02:23 INFO  req_8f26 GET /api/orders 200 201ms
14:02:31 ERROR req_8f29 GET /api/orders 500 30001ms
  Error: Connection terminated due to connection timeout
      at Connection.<anonymous> (node_modules/pg/lib/client.js:132)
14:02:40 INFO  req_8f2b GET /api/orders 200 189ms
```

</Evidence>

<Evidence title="metrics panel">

```text
http.5xx            flat at ~0 until 13:58, then ~33% of /api/orders requests
db.connections      steady climb from 13:40, flat-lines at 60 from 13:58
db.pool.waiting     0 until 13:58, then sawtooth 5–12
deploys             none today
cron                13:40  monthly-invoice-backfill  STARTED (still running)
```

</Evidence>

Work the steps: the error names its layer in plain text. The timeline has a sharp corner at 13:58 and an event at 13:40. There was no deploy. What changed?

<Reveal label="Reveal the cause — after you've committed to an answer">

**Layer:** data. The stack trace says so twice — `pg` and `pg-pool` are the Postgres client and its <Term k="connection-pool">connection pool</Term>. The app is healthy; it's waiting for a database connection that never arrives.

**Timeline:** errors start at 13:58. `db.connections` flat-lines at exactly 60 — the pool's ceiling — at the same moment. The climb started at 13:40.

**What changed at 13:40:** the `monthly-invoice-backfill` cron started, and it's still running. It has been steadily taking database connections and not releasing them fast enough. At 13:58 it had starved the pool: now any web request that can't grab a connection waits 30 seconds and dies. Two out of three requests sneak in when a connection frees up — which is why the failure is intermittent.

**Why "add retry logic" is the wrong fix:** retries would add *more* demand for connections to a starved pool — the same mechanism, made worse, plus hidden latency. It treats the symptom (a request failed) while feeding the cause (connection exhaustion). The agent wasn't wrong about the error; it was answering with one line of context instead of the timeline you now have.

**Cause-level fixes:** stop or throttle the backfill now (mitigation), then make it use a small dedicated connection budget and process in chunks (M7 covers this pattern), and alert on `db.pool.waiting > 0`.

</Reveal>

</Section>

<Section kind="ask-agent">

The method changes *how you brief the agent*: you hand it evidence and a layer, not a lone error line, and you verify against the reproduction.

<AgentTask prompt={"Production incident. Evidence: [paste the FULL stack trace, the first-occurrence timestamp, and the deploy/config history around it]. Based on this evidence only, which layer is failing — client, network, app, data, or third party — and what are the top 2 candidate causes? Do not propose a fix yet."}>
A good answer names the layer **and cites which line of your evidence says so**. If it proposes a fix anyway, or its layer contradicts the stack trace, push back — you've caught it pattern-matching.
</AgentTask>

<AgentTask prompt={"Here is the cause we identified: [cause]. Propose the smallest fix that addresses this mechanism, and tell me: (1) exactly which files you will change, (2) what could break as a side effect, (3) the command or request I can run to confirm the fix."}>
Before applying anything, read the diff: does it touch only the files it named? Then run its step (3) yourself — and also re-run **your own** reproduction from step 1. If the agent's confirmation and yours disagree, yours wins.
</AgentTask>

<AgentTask prompt={"Review this fix you just made: does it address the cause or suppress the symptom? Explain the failure mechanism in one sentence and why this change stops it. If you cannot, say so."}>
This is the one-sentence test from the mental model, turned on the agent. A real cause-fix survives this question; a patch produces a paragraph of hedging. Watch for the smell words: "should help", "adds resilience", "handles the edge case gracefully".
</AgentTask>

</Section>

<Section kind="checklist">

Questions to ask (yourself, your agent, or your engineer) during any incident:

- "What is the reproduction?" — if nobody can state it, you're guessing.
- "What does the **first** error in the logs say, in full?"
- "When exactly did it start, and what changed within an hour of that?"
- "Which layer, and which evidence says so?"
- "Does this fix stop the mechanism, or hide the signal?" — demand the one-sentence explanation.
- "How do we know it's fixed?" — the answer must be the reproduction passing, not "the errors stopped."

What good looks like on a team (or a one-person company): every incident produces a three-line writeup; nobody deploys a fix they can't explain; "let's add a retry" is a decision made *after* the cause is known, never instead of knowing it.

Red flag you can now catch in anyone — human, agent, or vendor support: a fix proposed before a layer is named.

</Section>
